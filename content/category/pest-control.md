## What pest control needs that general field service software does not

Three things separate this trade from cleaning and lawn care, and all three are reasons a general purpose scheduling tool can look adequate in a demo and fail in the second year.

Chemical application records come first. You need what was applied, where, at what rate, by whom, under which licence, and you need it retrievable years later. This is a regulatory obligation rather than a convenience, and it is the requirement that most often forces an operator off general software and onto something built for the trade.

Device and station tracking comes second, and it matters as soon as you take commercial work. Bait stations and monitors are physical assets at a location, inspected on a schedule, and their history belongs to the station rather than to the customer. A commercial client auditing your service will ask for the station record, not the invoice.

Recurring service agreements come third. Pest control sells a year of quarterly visits rather than a visit. The agreement renews, it can be paused over winter, and the billing is often monthly against quarterly service. Software that models a job but not an agreement will hold your schedule and misrepresent your revenue.

Jobber does none of the first two. It is a strong general field service product, it is the cheapest credible option at ten users in this comparison, and it has no chemical application log and no device tracking. That is the reason to think carefully before choosing it despite its price advantage at scale.

## The two products you can actually price

GorillaDesk publishes Basic at $49, Pro at $99 and Growth at $149 a month. The customer portal, which is the online booking component, begins on Pro, so $99 is the honest entry price rather than the $49 a competing comparison page will quote you.

GorillaDesk then charges per schedule rather than per user, which is the single most important thing to understand about its pricing. Its documentation describes a schedule as one technician's route, and states that where technicians work in crews only the crew leader needs a schedule. Each additional schedule is $50 a month.

The table above carries no figure in the GorillaDesk crew columns, because headcount is not what it bills for. On the crew leader basis its help centre describes, a crew of three riding in one vehicle is one schedule at $99. Ten people working as four crews is four schedules, which is $99 plus three at $50, or $249 a month.

Pest control is the trade where that assumption is least safe. Pest technicians commonly drive alone, and ten solo technicians means ten schedules, which is $99 plus nine at $50, or $549 a month. Work out how many vehicles leave your yard each morning before you use either figure, because the answer moves the bill by three hundred dollars a month.

Jobber publishes Core at $49, Connect at $139, Grow at $199 for five users and Plus at $499 for fifteen, with additional users at $29. Automated client reminders begin on Connect, so $139 is the honest entry price for anyone who does not intend to make confirmation calls by hand.

At ten users Jobber Connect is $299 on its ten user band, which is cheaper than the $344 that Grow costs at the same headcount and cheaper than the $400 that adding nine individual seats to the one user plan costs. The higher tier is the cheaper purchase, and the pricing page does not point that out.

## Why the other four decline to publish

Four of the six products most often recommended to small pest control operators route pricing through a sales conversation. Each has a different reason and each tells you something useful about how you would be sold to.

FieldRoutes prices on the number of active customers. It publishes two plan names, Growth and Corporate, and no figures, directing visitors to request pricing. Customer count based pricing means your software bill rises as your route grows, which is defensible and worth knowing before you sign rather than after.

PestPac is modular, and reporting on it consistently describes each module as carrying its own per user cost. A modular quote can be reasonable for a narrow need and expensive once the basic service functions are added back in. WorkWave shows no price on its pest control page and routes buyers to a demo booking and a sales line.

Briostack publishes a detailed page of add on and migration charges, including four cents a text message and $900 to consolidate a database, and no base subscription price anywhere on it. Knowing what the extras cost without knowing what the platform costs is an unusual way round, and it is why the table reads Could not confirm rather than Quote only.

ServSuite is now ServSuite by FieldRoutes, following ServiceTitan's acquisition of ServicePro and FieldRoutes, and is positioned as an enterprise solution. Its own domain no longer resolves to a product site. A twenty person operator is below the size it is sold to.

## What a chemical record has to hold

This is the requirement that separates pest control from every other trade on this site, and it is worth being specific about, because a feature list saying chemical tracking can mean very little.

A defensible application record holds the product name and its registration number, the concentration and the dilution rate, the quantity applied, the target pest, the treated area within the property, the date and time, the technician and their licence number, and the weather conditions where the application was outdoors.

It has to be captured at the property rather than reconstructed in the evening, and it has to be retrievable years later, because years later is when it will be asked for. A record assembled from memory after the fact is the kind of record that fails an audit.

Jobber holds none of this. It is a capable field service system with strong scheduling, quoting and invoicing, and it has no chemical application log and no device tracking. An operator running Jobber keeps application records somewhere else, which in practice means paper or a spreadsheet.

That is the trade you are making when you choose Jobber for pest control on price. It is a real trade rather than a disqualification, and plenty of small operators make it deliberately in their first year while the work is residential and the volume is low. It stops being viable the moment you take a commercial contract, because commercial clients audit.

GorillaDesk includes chemical and material tracking, which is the main reason it sits at the top of the table despite costing more than Jobber at one user.

## The twelve month number

Monthly pricing understates the gap between these two products, so it is worth annualising at the tier that includes online booking.

A solo operator pays $1,188 a year on GorillaDesk Pro against $1,668 on Jobber Connect. GorillaDesk is $480 a year cheaper before any question of features.

Three technicians riding as one crew pay $1,188 on GorillaDesk against $2,388 on Jobber Connect, a difference of $1,200 a year. Three technicians in three separate vehicles pay $2,388 on GorillaDesk, which is within a rounding error of Jobber and removes the cost argument entirely.

Ten people working as four crews pay $2,988 on GorillaDesk against $3,588 on Jobber Connect. Ten solo technicians pay $6,588 on GorillaDesk, which is the most expensive outcome anywhere in this comparison and $3,000 a year more than the alternative.

So the ranking between these two products is decided by your vehicle count rather than your headcount. Where crews ride together GorillaDesk is substantially cheaper at every size. Where every technician drives alone it becomes the most expensive option at ten. Across that whole range it is the only one of the two that holds a chemical application record, which for most operators settles the question regardless of the arithmetic.

The other four products cannot be placed on that scale at all, because none of them publishes a price.

## How the four pricing models behave as you hire

The six products in this table use four different pricing models, and the model matters more than the headline figure once you pass about five staff.

Per schedule, which is GorillaDesk, tracks vehicles rather than people. It is the cheapest model in this comparison for a crew based operation and the most expensive for a solo driver operation, and the same published price list produces both outcomes. Nothing in the vendor's headline figure tells you which one you are.

Banded per seat, which is Jobber, steps at thresholds. It is why ten users costs $299 on the ten user band rather than the $400 that nine additional seats would imply. The practical consequence is that the month you hire your sixth or eleventh technician the bill moves in a step rather than a slope, and the step can be downward if you switch band deliberately.

Per active customer, which is FieldRoutes, indexes the software bill to the size of your route rather than the size of your team. For a growing residential operation this is the model that compounds fastest, because a route that doubles doubles the software cost whether or not you hired anyone.

Modular per user, which is PestPac, multiplies two variables at once. Adding a technician raises the cost of every module you licence, so the effective per head cost depends on how many modules you have already bought.

An operator under twenty staff should know which of these four they are signing into before they compare any headline number, because at three technicians all four models produce similar figures and at fifteen they do not.

## Where each tool is the wrong choice

**GorillaDesk is wrong** for ten solo technicians on cost, where per schedule pricing reaches $549 a month and behaves exactly like per user pricing while still being described as per schedule.

**Jobber is wrong** for any operator who needs a chemical application log or bait station history. It does not have them. No price advantage compensates for a missing regulatory record, and this is the clearest wrong choice in the table.

**Briostack is wrong** for anyone who needs to compare cost before committing time to a sales process, because the only pricing it publishes is for the extras.

**FieldRoutes is wrong** for a small operator on principle. Pricing that scales with active customer count means the software cost grows with the route, and for a company under twenty staff that is the wrong direction to have built in.

**PestPac is wrong** for a simple recurring residential route. Evaluating it costs you a sales process before you learn whether it is affordable, which is a poor trade when the requirement is straightforward.

**ServSuite by FieldRoutes is wrong** at this size entirely. It is an enterprise product and a twenty person operator is below the size it is sold to.

## How to choose, in the order that matters

Take the questions in this order, because the first one that returns a hard answer ends the comparison.

Do you hold, or intend to bid for, commercial contracts? If yes, you need device and station history, and that removes Jobber. Compliance requirements are not negotiable in the way that a monthly price is.

How many vehicles leave your yard each morning? This is the question that prices GorillaDesk, and it is not the same as how many people you employ. Count vehicles, not staff.

Are you willing to enter a sales process to learn a number? If not, the comparison is between two products rather than six, and the four that decline to publish have removed themselves.

Only then compare the monthly figure. Most operators who work through those three questions in that order find that the price was the least decisive of the four.

## Common questions

**What is the best pest control software for a small company?**
GorillaDesk at $99 a month on the Pro plan, because it includes the customer portal, tracks chemicals and devices, and publishes a price you can budget from. Jobber at $139 is stronger operationally but has no chemical or device records, which rules it out for commercial work.

**How much does pest control software cost?**
The two products that publish a figure cost $99 and $139 a month at one technician, on the lowest tier that includes online booking. At ten technicians the published figures run from $249 to $549 depending on how many vehicles you run, and the other four products in this comparison do not publish a price at all.

**Why will most pest control software not show a price?**
Because they route it through a sales conversation. FieldRoutes states pricing is based on active customer count and asks you to request a figure. WorkWave shows no price for PestPac and offers a demo. Briostack publishes add on charges but no base subscription. ServSuite offers a quote and a demo. This is common in the category and it is a real cost in evaluation time.

**Can I use general field service software for pest control?**
You can run the scheduling and invoicing side. You cannot run the compliance side, because general tools have no chemical application log and no device tracking. Many small operators start on general software and move when a commercial contract requires station records.

**How current are these prices?**
They were read from vendor pricing pages in August 2026. Pricing in this category changes several times a year, and four of the six route pricing through a sales conversation rather than showing a figure we could read.
